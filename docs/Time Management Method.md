# Motivation
Throughout my entire life I've found myself struggling to manage my time. I would find myself setting very ambitious goals only to find, as time passes, that I am not getting any closer to achieving them. This would often cause me to be disheartened - not understanding how there are some people who just seem to be able to achieve so much more than I can, despite us having the same amount of time at our disposal.

I've tried many different methods for time management - but none of them seemed to work. Some methods, I found, were much to rigid. I would succeed in following a strict regiment for a month, maybe two, but then life got in the way. One "cheat day" turned in to two, which then turned into three and so on. On the other hand, I have learned that if one wishes to achieve his or her goals - he or she must be very intentional about time management. There is no use to having a vague idea of wanting to learn this language, or wanting to read this many books without deciding upon exactly how much time you would like to spend pursuing these goals (often, I find, the lack of willingness to be explicit about time allotment helps mask the painful fact that the goals are unrealistic and simply cannot be attained within a reasonable time frame).

These experiences led me to develop a method that is on the one hand very rigid in that it forces one to be very explicit about the amount of time performing each activity, while on the other hand allowing flexibility in when that time is spent.

Before going into the method itself I should mention - it is very possible that someone else has already thought of this method before me. Beyond very simple google searches I did not make any efforts to see if this method (or large parts of it) has already been invented. That being said, I came up with it out of true necessity and am hopeful that my description and development of it might be of some use.

After you have set your goals, you must perform the unenviable task of ranking them. It's very hard, I know, but it will be crucial when you are inevitably faced with dilemmas about how much time you want to spend working towards each of them. This also provides a great opportunity to ask yourself - why do you want to perform each goal? How important to you is it to achieve each goal?

Besides setting a clear goal that is measurable, you should also set certain control points to measure your progress. A lack of control points makes it very hard to see how you're doing until it's too late. For example, say I want to read 20 books during the next year. This is a good goal, since it is very easy to know if you have achieved it or not (lets say I've also selected the list of 20 specific books I want to read in advance, to not allow for selection of very short books in order to meed the deadline). Since the goal is 20 books during the next year, its quite simple to define quarterly control points: After three months I should have read 5 books. After six months I should have read 10 etc. If I reach 3 months having read only 2 books it means one of three things: 

1. The goal I set is not realistic.
2. I did not allot enough time in order to achieve this goal (see next step).
3. I did not follow my plan.

Regardless of which one it is - setting control points allows you to know that something is wrong relatively early.

## Step 2: Deriving Time Slots

After setting your goals, you must decide how much time you're willing to spend on them. The way to do this according to this method, is by defining *habits*.

A habit is simply a certain amount of time allotted to the performance of a task on a weekly or a daily basis. That is, according to this method there are two types of *habits*: daily and weekly. To take the example given earlier, I could define that I wish to read 30 minutes every day. Alternatively I could define that I wish to read 4 hours every week. 

Define such habits for every one of your goals (there could be more than one per goal!).

A tip for setting habits: don't make the number out of thin air, try to think how much time you would realistically need in order to achieve your goals. After the process is finished sum up the daily time and multiply it by 7. Add the total weekly habit time to get the total amount of time in a week you are expected to spend on your goals. Is the number that you got not feasable for the amount of free time you expect to have (always remember to leave buffers)? You probably need to adjust your goals.

Now all that's left to do is to actually execute your plan. Luckily the method also includes a proposal for how to carry this out. This consists of three types of planning\review sessions you hold with yourself:

### Weekly Planning and Review Session
There are two parts to this session: *review* and *planning*. During the review phase you analyze the previous week and ask yourself:
* Did I manage to perform my habits as planned?
* Do I feel like I've progressed this week towards achieving my goal?
* Did I spend my habit time effectively (meaning, was I completely devoted to performing the tasks, without distractions)?
If the answer to any of these questions is no, ask yourself why and what are the changes you must implement so that next week will be better.
